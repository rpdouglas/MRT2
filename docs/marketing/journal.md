# My Journal — Marketing & Persona Brief

**What this document is for:** briefing material for an LLM writing marketing copy (ads, landing pages, App/Play Store copy, email, social) about MRT's Journal feature. It explains what the Journal actually does, why that matters clinically in substance-use recovery, and exactly how each of MRT's six user personas uses it in real life — so copy comes out grounded in the real product and real recovery science, not generic wellness-app language.

This is the first in a per-feature series. MRT is a multi-module recovery companion — Journal, Tasks, Vitality, Workbooks, CBT/DBT Tools, Recovery Games, Insights, and Profile all work together as one system. This brief focuses on Journal but includes a short "how it connects" section at the end so copy about Journal doesn't contradict or ignore the rest of the app.

---

## The one-sentence pitch

**My Journal is a private, AI-assisted place to process what's actually happening in early recovery — text, voice, or guided prompts — that no one else, including MRT, can read.**

---

## Why journaling matters in addiction recovery

This isn't a generic "gratitude journal" feature bolted onto a habit tracker. Structured self-reflection is one of the most consistently used tools across every major recovery framework, for reasons that map directly onto what the Journal does:

- **12-Step programs** (AA, NA, CA): Step 4 ("a searching and fearless moral inventory") and Step 10 (ongoing daily inventory) are, structurally, journaling practices. Sponsors routinely ask sponsees to write about resentments, fears, and cravings between meetings.
- **CBT / SMART Recovery**: thought records — noticing a trigger, the automatic thought, the emotion, and a more balanced response — are a core cognitive-restructuring technique. Writing it down is what makes the pattern visible.
- **Relapse prevention research**: self-monitoring of mood, cravings, and triggers is one of the most replicated tools for helping someone spot a relapse warning sign before it becomes a relapse, not after.
- **MAT (Medication-Assisted Treatment)**: correlating cravings, sleep, and side effects against dose timing is standard behavioral-health practice for anyone stabilizing on Suboxone or Naltrexone.

The Journal is where all of that actually happens. Voice-to-text and mood tracking exist because in early recovery, "just write about it" is often too much friction at the exact moment writing about it would help most.

**Marketing framing:** this is a recovery *practice* the app makes easier to sustain — not a feature invented by a product team with no connection to how recovery actually works.

## What the Journal actually does

### Write — three ways in, on purpose
- **Free text** — a distraction-free page, no character limit.
- **Guided templates** — structured prompts (morning intention, evening inventory, gratitude, trigger log) for someone who doesn't know where to start, or a custom prompt set they built for their own program.
- **Voice-to-Vault** — speak it instead of typing it. The app transcribes it, scores the mood, and tags it automatically. This exists specifically for the moment when someone is too overwhelmed, too tired, or too shaky to type — the barrier to "get it out of your head" drops to almost nothing.

Every entry also carries a 1–10 mood slider and free-form tags, so emotional data accumulates automatically, without extra effort, just by journaling normally.

### History — the whole story, searchable
Every past entry, organized by year and month, instantly searchable. Not a scattered pile of notes app entries — a continuous, private record of the recovery journey, from day one forward.

### Insights — patterns you can't see day to day
Mood trends over time, a day-of-week rhythm comparison, and a word cloud of what actually shows up most in someone's own writing. No AI required for this tab — it's the person's own data, reflected back.

### AI Pattern Analysis — an on-demand recovery coach
On request, the app reads someone's own journal history and surfaces what a human might take months to notice: recurring themes, emotional trajectory, early warning signs, and concrete suggested next steps — which can be pushed straight into their task list. Available as a weekly check-in, a monthly review, or a 90-day deep dive for spotting long-arc patterns.

### The privacy promise (this is the headline differentiator)
Every journal entry is encrypted on the person's own device before it ever reaches a server, using a key **derived from their own PIN — nobody else has it, including MRT.** Even in the worst-case scenario of a server breach, journal content is unreadable. This isn't a privacy *policy* — it's math. The plain-language version for copy: **"Not even we can read what you write."**

This matters enormously for this specific audience: people in early recovery are writing about relapses, cravings, resentments, sponsor conversations, sometimes things they haven't told anyone. The honesty the practice requires is only possible if the privacy is real, not promised.

## How each persona uses the Journal

MRT designs around six real recovery personas (full detail in `docs/PERSONAS.md`). Here's specifically how each one uses Journal — use these as the basis for testimonial-style copy, use-case sections, or persona-targeted ad variants. Do not invent details beyond what's here or in the personas doc; if a claim needs a specific stat or quote, flag it rather than fabricating one.

### David — "The Survivor" (Day 1–30, Cocaine Anonymous)
> *"I just need the noise to stop right now."*

David relapsed after two years clean and is in acute crisis — high anxiety, cognitively overloaded, often journaling (if at all) alone at 2 AM. For David, typing a paragraph is too much. He uses **Voice-to-Vault**: he talks, the app transcribes and tags it, and the act of getting the thought out of his head and somewhere safe is the entire value — not eloquence, not structure. The mood slider gives him a way to log "this is bad" without finding words. He is never asked to reflect on patterns in the moment; that's not his job right now.

**Marketing angle:** Journal as a pressure-release valve at 2 AM, for someone who can't handle one more app that expects a lot from him. Never pair David's story with streaks, stats, or "keep it up" language — none of that belongs to his moment.

### Ned — "The Pink Cloud" (Day 30–90, Narcotics Anonymous)
> *"I'm going to crush it today!"*

Ned is riding early-recovery momentum and treats his recovery like a fitness app — but around Day 90, the euphoria fades and this is his highest-relapse-risk window. Early on, Ned journals quickly between tasks, tags things, moves on. Around Day 90, MRT deliberately surfaces the **AI Pattern Analysis** for the first time: *"You've logged 90 days — here's what your data shows."* This is the moment Journal stops being a box to check and starts being the deeper tool that catches him when the gamification alone won't be enough.

**Marketing angle:** the app that has something real waiting for you when the initial high of early recovery fades — not just another streak to maintain.

### Lisa — "The Service Superstar" (7 years, Alcoholics Anonymous, sponsors 3–6 women)
> *"I have five sponsees and a full-time job. I need to stay organized so I don't burn out."*

Lisa's own Journal use is squeezed into five minutes in her car after a meeting, before the next thing. She's not doing deep reflection here — she's using it as her own inventory practice, the counterbalance to a life spent managing everyone else's recovery. For Lisa, Journal is the one place that's about *her* program, not her sponsees'.

**Marketing angle:** even the person holding everyone else up needs somewhere private that's just for her.

### Walt — "The Zen Master" (35+ years, AA-origin, now Recovery Dharma)
> *"Recovery is a lifelong practice of mindfulness and reflection."*

Walt is Journal's power user. Morning coffee, no interruptions, 800-word entries, on a tablet. No character limits, no gamification anywhere near his flow, and every AI insight must show exactly which entries it's drawing from — he will not trust output he can't trace. He uses the 90-day Deep Dive and full data export (JSON/PDF) because he owns his data and expects to keep it, on his terms, indefinitely.

**Marketing angle:** for someone who has been doing this for decades, Journal is a serious long-form writing practice with real data sovereignty — not a gamified habit tracker wearing a journal's clothes.

### Maya — "The Systematiser" (6–18 months, SMART Recovery / secular / CBT)
> *"I want to understand the mechanics of my brain so I can upgrade my operating system."*

Maya treats recovery like a curriculum and journaling like data collection. She wants her mood and trigger data cross-referenced precisely against 90-day windows, and she will stop trusting an AI insight the moment it can't show its source entries. Her Insights tab and the AI Pattern Analysis Wizard are core daily tools, not occasional check-ins.

**Marketing angle:** for the person who wants to see the actual evidence behind "you're improving" — not a vague affirmation, a traceable pattern in her own words.

### Jordan — "The Stabiliser" (Day 1–12mo+, MAT/Buprenorphine or Naltrexone + MARA/SMART)
> *"My recovery combines medicine and behavior. I need tools that support my physical stability without judgment."*

Jordan is stabilizing on medication-assisted treatment and has been burned by abstinence-only environments that treated their medication as "not really sober." Jordan uses journal tags and mood tracking to correlate cravings and side effects against dose timing — quietly, without the app ever implying their recovery path is less legitimate. Discretion matters: nothing in Jordan's journaling flow should ever surface medication names on a lock screen or imply judgment about their path.

**Marketing angle:** a private place to track what's actually happening in your recovery, without a single line of copy anywhere implying medication-assisted recovery is a lesser path. This audience has been shamed by other tools and other rooms — do not repeat it.

## How Journal connects to the rest of MRT

Journal isn't an island — copy about it can (accurately) gesture at the bigger system:

- **AI Pattern Analysis can create Tasks directly** — a suggested action from a journal analysis becomes a to-do item on the Dashboard/Tasks screen, with a visible link back to the insight that generated it.
- **Journal entries feed the Insights/Recovery Capital picture** alongside Workbooks and Vitality data — mood, sleep, and workbook progress build one picture of a person's recovery over time, not separate silos.
- **The same zero-knowledge privacy model covers the whole app** — Journal, Workbooks, and sponsor notes are all encrypted the same way. "Not even we can read it" is a whole-product promise, not a Journal-only feature.
- **CBT/DBT tool sessions (ABC, CBA, Thought Record, etc.) can be saved as journal entries** — so a completed guided exercise becomes part of the same searchable history, not a disconnected worksheet.

## Brand voice & marketing guardrails

This is a recovery product for people in a vulnerable state. Copy generated from this brief must hold to these rules without exception:

- **Never make clinical or medical claims.** MRT is a peer-support companion tool, not a treatment, therapy, or medical device. Never imply it can diagnose, treat, or cure addiction or any co-occurring condition.
- **Never use shame, fear, or scarcity language.** No "don't let addiction win," no relapse-shaming, no countdown-to-failure framing. This mirrors a hard product rule: MRT never uses red "failure" states or "overdue" language anywhere in the app, and marketing copy should hold the same line.
- **Respect every recovery path as legitimate**, in the same sentence if needed: 12-Step (AA/NA/CA), SMART Recovery, Recovery Dharma, secular/CBT approaches, and medication-assisted recovery (MAT). Never imply MAT is "not really sober" — this is a documented harm this audience has already experienced elsewhere.
- **Lead with privacy, honestly.** "Zero-knowledge encryption" and "not even we can read it" are true, verifiable claims — use them as a real differentiator, not a vague "we take privacy seriously" line every app uses.
- **Never fabricate testimonials, statistics, or user quotes.** The persona vignettes above are composite design personas, not real users — do not present them as real customer testimonials.
- **Don't overclaim the AI.** It surfaces patterns in someone's own writing; it doesn't predict relapse, diagnose anything, or replace a sponsor or therapist.
- **Crisis-safe by default.** Any copy that could reach someone in acute crisis (app store description, first-run ad, onboarding email) should never assume the reader is stable, motivated, or having a good day — see David's persona above. When in doubt, write for 2 AM, not for the best-case user.
