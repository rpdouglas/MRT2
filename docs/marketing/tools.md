# My Tools — Marketing & Persona Brief

**What this document is for:** briefing material for an LLM writing marketing copy about MRT's Tools — the CBT/SMART Recovery worksheet library plus two standalone crisis tools. It explains what Tools actually does, why the specific exercises matter clinically, and how each persona uses it — so copy comes out grounded in real, established recovery techniques, not generic "coping tools" language.

Third in the per-feature series (`docs/marketing/journal.md`, `docs/marketing/dashboard.md` before this; `docs/marketing/README.md` tracks the rest). Built on `docs/screens/tools/` after spot-verifying its claims directly against source — see the "Known gaps" note near the end for the one thing worth being careful with in copy.

## The one-sentence pitch

**My Tools is a library of real CBT and SMART Recovery exercises, organized by the moment you're actually in — not a clinical menu, a response to what's happening right now.**

---

## Why these specific tools matter in recovery

This isn't a generic "coping skills" grab-bag. Every tool here is a digitized version of an established technique:

- **Urge Surfing** — the crisis tool, Urge Surfer, is built on a real, well-studied relapse-prevention technique: treating a craving as a wave to ride out rather than a command to obey. It doesn't try to make the urge disappear; it helps someone stay with it until it passes on its own, which it reliably does.
- **CBA (Cost-Benefit Analysis), ABC, D.E.N.T.S., Thought Record** — core SMART Recovery and CBT worksheets, not invented exercises. These are the same structured tools a SMART Recovery meeting or a CBT-oriented therapist would actually use — MRT just makes them available at 2 AM, alone, without waiting for the next session.
- **Organized by moment, not by clinical name.** Nobody in a hard moment thinks "I need Cognitive Behavioral Therapy techniques" — they think "I need something right now" or "I want to plan ahead of this happening again." Structuring the whole library around that (Right Now / Before It Happens / After a Hard Moment / Big Picture) matches how a craving or a hard moment actually unfolds in a real day.

**Marketing framing:** "real technique, made usable in the moment" — not wellness-app coping cards, actual clinical tools someone can reach for at 2 AM instead of only in a scheduled session.

## What Tools actually does

### Organized by the moment you're in
Four sections, each collapsed until tapped so the screen isn't overwhelming:

- **Right Now** — for cravings and crisis: Urge Surfer, Resentment Burner.
- **Before It Happens** — planning ahead of a risky moment: D.E.N.T.S., Cost-Benefit Analysis, Morning Intent.
- **After a Hard Moment** — processing what just happened: ABC Coping, Personify & Disarm, Thought Record, Five Questions.
- **Big Picture** — stepping back to plan: Lifestyle Balance.

### Two kinds of tools
- **Two quick, no-pressure tools** (Urge Surfer, Resentment Burner) — open them, use them, done. Resentment Burner in particular saves nothing at all; it's built to be used and let go of, on purpose.
- **Nine structured worksheets**, most walking someone through a real exercise step by step, with progress saved automatically and resumable later — pick up exactly where you left off, even on a different day. Every completed one shows a "Completed N times" count and a plain-language history of past answers, so returning to a tool isn't starting from zero.

### Built crisis-safe, not just crisis-labeled
Urge Surfer is the one tool in the entire app reachable even when the vault is locked — no PIN needed to reach it. And in the rare case someone's using it mid-crisis with the vault locked, the app still saves what they wrote rather than losing it, even though that specific save can't be encrypted in that moment. This isn't a shortcut the team overlooked — it's a deliberate choice, made explicitly for the person having the hardest night: capturing what they're going through matters more, in that one moment, than the encryption promise that governs everything else. (See the guardrail below on how to talk about this carefully — it's a real and moving detail, but an easy one to state clumsily.)

### AI coaching, when it's useful
On the guided worksheets, Premium unlocks two small AI-assisted moments: a gentle follow-up "AI Suggestion" question if someone's stuck mid-answer, and — on the Cost-Benefit Analysis tool specifically — a one-sentence reflection summarizing what the completed exercise actually shows. Both are optional add-ons to a tool that already works fully without them.

### Every answer becomes part of the same private record
A completed tool isn't a separate, disconnected worksheet — it's saved with the same encryption as a journal entry, and shows up in Journal History and the AI Pattern Analysis right alongside freeform entries. Working through a Thought Record on a hard Tuesday becomes part of the same searchable, private record as everything else someone's written.

## How each persona uses Tools

MRT designs around six real recovery personas (full detail in `docs/PERSONAS.md`). Do not invent details beyond what's here or in the personas doc.

### David — "The Survivor" (Day 1–30, Cocaine Anonymous)
> *"I just need the noise to stop right now."*

David's tool is Urge Surfer, specifically, and specifically *because* it doesn't require unlocking anything first. No PIN, no vault, no delay between "I need this" and "I'm using it." It's the one place in Tools built entirely for his worst-case moment, not his best one.

**Marketing angle:** the crisis tool that's actually reachable *during* a crisis — not gated behind the same steps as everything else.

### Ned — "The Pink Cloud" (Day 30–90, Narcotics Anonymous)
> *"I'm going to crush it today!"*

Ned isn't Tools' primary audience early on, but around his Day 90 crash — when the gamification that carried him starts to feel hollow — this is exactly what MRT surfaces next: real structured tools instead of another streak.

**Marketing angle:** consistent with the Journal and Dashboard briefs — the deeper thing waiting for him when the initial high fades.

### Lisa — "The Service Superstar" (7 years, Alcoholics Anonymous, sponsors 3–6 women)
> *"I have five sponsees and a full-time job. I need to stay organized so I don't burn out."*

Tools isn't Lisa's primary story — her persona documentation doesn't name a specific tool she leans on. Don't invent a Tools-specific angle for her; her marketing story belongs to Journal and the Service side of the app.

### Walt — "The Zen Master" (35+ years, AA-origin, now Recovery Dharma)
> *"Recovery is a lifelong practice of mindfulness and reflection."*

The structured, worksheet-style format fits Walt's demand for traceable, auditable content — a completed CBA or Thought Record is a clear, specific artifact he can look back on, not a vague mood entry. He's not the primary user of the AI-coaching add-ons (per his general "no black-box AI" stance elsewhere in the app), but the tools themselves, used plainly, fit his practice well.

**Marketing angle:** structured self-work with a clear paper trail — not gamified, not vague.

### Maya — "The Systematiser" (6–18 months, SMART Recovery / secular / CBT)
> *"I want to understand the mechanics of my brain so I can upgrade my operating system."*

Maya is Tools' primary audience. Per her own persona documentation, she's frustrated by vague advice and specifically prefers "rigorous CBT toolsets like CBA and DENTS" — this is, almost word for word, what Tools is built to be. The completion counts and resumable structure match her systematic, curriculum-style approach to recovery.

**Marketing angle:** this is the strongest, most literal persona-to-feature match in the whole app — lean on it directly.

### Jordan — "The Stabiliser" (Day 1–12mo+, MAT/Buprenorphine or Naltrexone + MARA/SMART)
> *"My recovery combines medicine and behavior. I need tools that support my physical stability without judgment."*

Jordan's persona documentation explicitly calls for "non-spiritual, science-backed behavioral tools" — and Tools, unlike some of the rest of the app, carries no 12-Step or fellowship framing at all. It's CBT and SMART Recovery, full stop, which makes it one of the more naturally comfortable corners of MRT for someone whose path isn't rooted in the 12 Steps.

**Marketing angle:** behavioral science, not spiritual framework — worth saying directly for an audience that's been made to feel like their path needs translating elsewhere.

## How Tools connects to the rest of MRT

- **Every completed tool becomes a Journal entry** — same encryption, same History timeline, same eligibility for AI Pattern Analysis. Tools isn't a side feature; it's another way into the same private record.
- **The Tools tile lives on the Dashboard** — one tap from the home screen (see `docs/marketing/dashboard.md`).
- **AI Pattern Analysis can read a completed CBA or Thought Record** the same way it reads a freeform entry — a pattern spotted across someone's tool answers is treated the same as a pattern in their writing.

## Brand voice & marketing guardrails

Everything from `docs/marketing/journal.md`'s guardrails applies here too. Tools-specific additions:

- **These are self-help tools, not therapy.** Never describe them as treatment, counseling, or a replacement for a therapist — they're structured exercises a person can do on their own, the same as they'd find in a SMART Recovery workbook.
- **Handle the Urge Surfer crisis-save detail carefully.** It's real, verified, and genuinely moving — but it's a narrow, specific exception for one crisis tool in one locked-vault scenario, not a general statement about the app's privacy. Never let copy imply "MRT sometimes doesn't encrypt your data" as a general claim — the correct framing is "the one crisis tool that's built to capture what you're going through even in the worst-case scenario, because that matters more in that moment than anything else." This belongs in a deeper trust/security piece more than a headline claim.
- **Don't oversell the AI coaching.** It's a small, optional Premium add-on to tools that work fully without it — not the point of the feature.
- **Don't claim clinical outcomes.** "Reduce cravings," "interrupt automatic thoughts," and similar language should describe what the technique is designed to help with, not promise a guaranteed result.
